ZONE 01

Collection

Intake active

Where waste begins

Waste management starts at the point of use: food wrappers, hygiene supplies, maintenance materials, packaging, and wastewater from daily crew activities. In microgravity, air currents and sealed containers help keep loose debris from drifting through the cabin.

Crews separate materials where practical, seal them in bags or containers, and route them to designated stowage or processing equipment. Good labeling and inventory tracking prevent mix-ups and make later recovery easier.

Collection interfaces

  • Waste bags: Contain dry trash and reduce odor exposure.
  • Liquid lines: Route urine and other wastewater to treatment systems.
  • Condensate collection: Captures humidity from cabin air.
  • Compaction: Reduces the volume occupied by packaging and dry trash.

Collection is not just “throwing things away.” It protects cabin air quality, prevents contamination, preserves materials that may contain recoverable water, and keeps hazardous items separate from ordinary trash.

ZONE 02

Sorting

Material routing nominal

Separate, label, track

Sorting determines whether an item can be recycled, treated, compacted for storage, returned to Earth, disposed of overboard, or saved for a future processing technology. A spacecraft cannot rely on the enormous landfills and industrial recycling networks available on Earth.

Categories may include dry trash, wet trash, packaging, metals, plastics, hygiene waste, electronic waste, and hazardous materials. The goal is to keep recoverable resources available while preventing biological, chemical, or fire hazards.

ZONE 03

Processing

Thermal loop cycling

Water Processor

The International Space Station’s Water Recovery System collects wastewater—including urine and cabin humidity condensate—and sends it to the Water Processor Assembly for purification.

Treatment can include filtration, multifiltration beds, catalytic oxidation, ion exchange, and disinfection. NASA reports that the station system can recover and recycle about 90% of the water on station; with the Brine Processor Assembly demonstration, NASA has reported reaching a 98% total water-recovery goal.

Trash Compaction

Compaction reduces the volume of dry waste and packaging. NASA’s Trash Compaction and Processing System concept uses pressure and heat to remove water from trash while recovering that water and treating gas byproducts.

NASA describes TCPS as a technology development effort. Its projected benefits include recovered habitable volume, water recovered from trash, and material that could potentially be used for radiation shielding, but those projections should not be treated as routine operational results.

Sanitation

Hygiene waste, food residue, and wet materials can support microbial growth if left unmanaged. Sealed containment, drying or stabilization where appropriate, surface cleaning, and air filtration all help limit contamination.

On the ISS, trash is generally packed into resupply vehicles that either return to Earth or burn up during atmospheric reentry. Future deep-space missions will need more on-board stabilization and resource recovery because regular returns or disposal opportunities may not exist.

ZONE 04

Storage

Capacity monitoring

Every cubic meter matters

Stowage is a scarce resource aboard a spacecraft. Waste must be contained without blocking emergency paths, damaging equipment, creating odor, or adding unnecessary mass in the wrong location.

Crews track filled containers, compact materials when possible, and schedule transfers to cargo vehicles. On long-duration missions, storage planning becomes part of mission design: the facility must know not only how much waste exists, but what it contains and what could still be recovered from it.

Storage priorities

  • Keep emergency routes and equipment access clear.
  • Contain odors, liquids, and microbial growth.
  • Separate hazardous or reactive materials.
  • Preserve recoverable water and useful materials.
  • Avoid allowing waste volume to displace crew supplies.
ZONE 05

Resource Recovery

Recovery systems online

Water: the highest-value loop

Water is one of the most important resources to recycle because launching it is expensive. A spacecraft can collect humidity condensate, urine, and other wastewater, then purify it for reuse.

NASA’s Environmental Control and Life Support System uses a Water Recovery System for this purpose. The agency reports that the station’s system recycles about 90% of water on station, and that the Brine Processor Assembly demonstration helped reach the 98% total water-recovery goal. [1][6]

Inventory reduces resupply

Recycling works best when crews know what they have. Tracking packaging, spare parts, tools, consumables, and waste streams lets a habitat reuse materials instead of launching replacements.

Careful inventory can also identify items that should be redesigned: less packaging, reusable containers, standardized parts, and materials that are easier to process all reduce the amount of waste a mission must manage.

Operating systems vs. future concepts

Demonstrated or operating

  • ISS wastewater collection and purification.
  • Water recovery from urine and cabin humidity condensate.
  • Trash packing and transfer aboard resupply vehicles.
  • Brine Processor Assembly demonstration for higher water recovery.

Research / proposed

  • Large-scale conversion of trash into construction or shielding materials.
  • Bioregenerative wastewater treatment for exploration habitats.
  • Thermochemical conversion of mixed waste into useful feedstocks.
  • In-space manufacturing using recycled materials.

NASA identifies trash compaction, thermochemical conversion, bioregenerative wastewater management, waste stabilization, and in-space manufacturing from recycled materials as active waste-management technology areas; several remain research or development efforts rather than mature operational systems. CREW OPS

Routines & Safety

Procedures available

Daily routines

  • Place dry trash in designated sealed bags.
  • Keep liquids and hygiene waste in proper collection systems.
  • Label and log unusual or hazardous items.
  • Clean contact surfaces and food-preparation areas.
  • Report leaks, odors, blocked filters, or damaged containers.

Odor and contamination

In a sealed habitat, small sources of odor or microbial growth can become noticeable quickly. Ventilation, filtration, sealed waste bags, regular cleaning, and prompt handling of wet waste help maintain a healthy cabin.

Contamination prevention also means keeping wastewater, food residue, cleaning chemicals, and maintenance debris separated whenever possible.

Emergencies

  • Leak: Contain liquid, protect electrical equipment, notify the crew.
  • Fire or smoke: Follow the vehicle’s fire-response procedure first.
  • Chemical spill: Isolate the area and use approved cleanup materials.
  • Blocked system: Stop the affected process and follow maintenance steps.
  • Loss of containment: Prioritize air quality, hygiene, and safe access.
DESIGN

Earth Orbit vs. Deep Space

Mission architecture

How waste handling changes with mission destination
Factor Earth-orbit spacecraft Moon / Mars habitats
Resupply Cargo vehicles can bring supplies and take packed trash away or dispose of it during reentry. Resupply is slow, expensive, or unavailable; habitats must retain and reuse more material.
Water The ISS already recovers most water from wastewater and humidity condensate. Recovery becomes even more critical, especially where local water extraction is difficult.
Trash Trash is commonly compacted, stored, and transferred to visiting cargo vehicles. Trash may need stabilization, volume reduction, and conversion into useful materials.
Maintenance Replacement parts and expertise may be available through frequent missions. Crews need robust equipment, spare parts, repair capability, and simpler servicing.
Safety Systems are designed around microgravity containment and rapid Earth-return options. Dust, radiation, partial gravity, communication delays, and limited evacuation options add constraints.
R&D BAY

Future Concepts

Research direction

CONCEPT

Waste into shielding

Compacted or processed trash could potentially become radiation-shielding material. NASA’s Trash Compaction and Processing System project estimates that a one-year, four-person mission could produce more than 900 kg of shielding tiles and recover water from trash, but this is a technology projection rather than an established operational capability. [2][4]

CONCEPT

Waste into feedstock

Thermochemical processing could break down mixed waste into gases, oils, char, or chemical feedstocks. These outputs might someday support manufacturing, but they require careful control of temperature, pressure, contaminants, and byproducts.

CONCEPT

Biological treatment

Future habitats may use biological systems alongside physical and chemical treatment to manage hygiene water and other wastewater. NASA’s Alternate Water Processor is an example of an integrated biological and physicochemical approach designed for exploration missions. [11]

The best space-waste system is not a single magical recycler. It is a chain of careful choices: generate less waste, capture valuable resources, contain hazards, maintain equipment, and design materials for reuse from the beginning.
